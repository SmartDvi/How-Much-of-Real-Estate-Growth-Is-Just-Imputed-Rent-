A plain-language guide

Is your country's
"real estate boom"
actually real?

Governments count the rent homeowners would pay themselves as part of the economy's real estate output — even though no one hands over a single coin. We pulled 20+ years of official data for 43 countries to find out how much of "real estate growth" is that pretend payment, and how much is people actually buying, selling, letting, and building.

65 out of every 100 dollars counted as "real estate output," in a typical country, is rent nobody actually paid.

What is "pretend rent," really?

If you own your home outright, you don't pay rent to anyone. But the people who compile a country's economic statistics don't like that — it would make owners and renters look incomparable. So they invent a number: what you would have paid yourself to live in your own house, as if you were your own landlord. No cash moves. No tenant exists. Nobody gets a payslip. But that invented number gets added to "real estate industry output," right alongside the money that actual estate agents, landlords, and property developers earn for real work.

Economists call it imputed rent. Everyone else could just call it phantom rent — it behaves like income on the national ledger, but it never once changes hands.

Why this should matter to you

If you rent

"Real estate sector grew 8% this year" does not mean landlords built more homes or rents got cheaper. Most of that number can be pure valuation. Watch the actual Rent Price Index for your city, not the sector total, if you want to know what's coming for your rent.

If you own your home

Yes, the government pretends you pay yourself rent. It changes nothing about your mortgage, your taxes, or your monthly budget. It only inflates how big the national economy looks on paper.

If you trade or manage property

Your real business is the "excluding phantom rent" slice, not the total. A rising sector headline is not proof business is picking up for you — in Czechia, the real, fee-earning side of the business actually shrank while the headline number climbed.

If you just read the news

Before believing "the property boom is powering the economy," ask: how much of that is phantom rent? On the typical country's numbers, assume about two-thirds — until shown otherwise.

What we actually found

1. It depends entirely on which country you live in

We expected a simple story: phantom rent creeping up everywhere. It isn't. In Czechia, it climbed from 61% of the real-estate total in 1990 to 70% in 2024. In the United Kingdom, it went the opposite way — from 80% down to 73% — because British letting agents, property managers, and developers grew their real business faster than the phantom-rent number did.

2. The sharpest finding: in Czechia, the "real" part is actually shrinking

Strip out the effect of rising prices and look at genuine activity — actual deals done, actual fees earned — and Czechia's non-phantom real estate business has been shrinking for over 30 years, even as the headline sector total kept climbing. Almost all of the reported "growth" is the phantom-rent valuation getting bigger on paper.

Picture a market stall that both sells real vegetables and keeps a "display shelf value" it writes down for produce it would theoretically sell to itself. In Czechia, actual vegetable sales have quietly shrunk for three decades — every headline about "growth" is the display-shelf number climbing, not more customers walking up to the stall.

3. Numbers can jump overnight for reasons that have nothing to do with the economy

In 2020, Slovakia's phantom-rent share leapt from 0% to about 80% in a single year. Did Slovak homeowners suddenly start valuing their houses eighty times higher? No — the statistics office simply started counting something it had always ignored before. Anyone glancing at the total "real estate" number that year would have seen what looks exactly like a spectacular property boom. It was a filing-cabinet decision, not a market one.

It's like a school that never graded handwriting suddenly making it 80% of the report card. The grade transforms overnight — the child's handwriting didn't.

4. We even asked a computer to predict it. It couldn't.

We fed a machine-learning model everything a reasonable person might guess would drive next year's phantom-rent share: house prices, rent inflation, how fast the whole economy was growing. Across three different modeling approaches — including a neural network, the same family of technology behind modern AI — none of them beat simply guessing "no change." That's a real result, not a failed experiment: it tells us that big swings in this number come from paperwork decisions (like Slovakia's), not from anything the everyday housing market is signaling in advance.

Big words, plainly explained

Imputed rent
The "pretend rent" a homeowner is scored as paying themselves. No cash moves — it's an accounting entry, not a payment.
Percentage point
If a share moves from 60% to 65%, that's "5 percentage points," not "5%" (which would mean 60 → 63). News headlines often blur this — watch for it.
Nominal vs. real
Nominal = the raw dollar amount, prices included. Real = with rising prices stripped out, showing whether actual activity grew — like asking if a shop got more customers, not just higher prices.
Median
Line every country up from lowest to highest — the median is whoever's standing exactly in the middle. Harder for one extreme country to distort than a plain average.
Correlation (r)
A score from -1 to +1 for "do these two things move together?" We found r ≈ 0.30 between phantom-rent growth and real rents — a loose link, not a tight one.
Statistically significant
Roughly, "the odds this pattern is just random noise are low." It's the standard bar researchers use before calling a pattern real.
Forecast band
The shaded zone around a forecast line. It means: "our best guess is the middle — but don't hold us to the exact number; anywhere in the shading wouldn't surprise us."

What to actually do about it

Pick the group that's you — each one gets specific, concrete moves, not just "be skeptical."

Renters

  • A rosy "real estate sector grew" headline is not a signal that more rentals are coming or that landlords are under pressure to lower rent — remember, ~65% of that growth can be phantom.
  • Track your city's actual Rent Price Index and vacancy rate for a real read on where your rent is headed — not the national sector total.

Homeowners

  • The phantom rent you're "scored" as paying yourself changes nothing about your mortgage, your take-home pay, or your taxes in most countries — don't let a rising national number make you feel richer or poorer than you actually are.
  • If you want to know if your area's real estate market is genuinely accelerating, look for actual sale prices and transaction counts near you, not the blended national sector figure.

Property traders & small businesses

  • Your real exposure is the "excluding phantom rent" slice. When the sector headline rises, check whether that's actually your segment (agents, lettings, management, development) or just the valuation side — Czechia shows these can move in completely opposite directions.
  • Track house-price and transaction-volume indices directly — they track the real, fee-earning side of the business noticeably better than the blended total does.

Everyday citizens & voters

  • Before accepting "the property boom is driving our economy" from a politician or headline, ask: how much of that is phantom rent? Assume roughly two-thirds until shown otherwise.
  • A sudden one-year jump in real-estate figures is more often a bookkeeping change than a sign a policy worked — don't let one year's number decide your vote on housing policy.

Journalists & analysts

  • Report "real estate sector growth" as two numbers, not one: the phantom-rent part and the genuine-activity part. Only the second one means jobs, transactions, or market strength.
  • Before running a "real estate boom" story off one year's data jump, check whether a country's statistics office changed methodology that year.

Policymakers & statistical offices

  • Publish the phantom/real split as standard practice, the way some European countries already do — don't make analysts request it specially.
  • Flag benchmark revisions and methodology changes prominently in the same release as the headline number, so a filing change is never mistaken for a market event.

Investors

  • Don't treat a rising aggregate real-estate GDP figure as a signal of brokerage, construction, or transaction-market strength on its own — check the non-phantom line, or proxy it with house-price/transaction indices.
  • Our forecasting tests found next year's swing in the phantom share is close to unpredictable from market data alone — don't build a trading thesis on calling its direction.
